In 1948 two law partners in Milwaukee, Elmer Winter and Aaron Scheinfeld, had to bring in temporary help to meet a deadline. So they started a company that sent people into offices for a day or a month. Manpower became one of the largest staffing companies in the world. What they sold needed no integration. It was a person, and every office already knew what to do with one.

78 years later the crew is ready. Models write, reason and code at a level few of us expected this soon, and they pass the Turing test in writing and in voice. Yet most people still use them as a smarter search box. The bottleneck has moved from intelligence to distribution.

In January 2007 none of the parts inside the first iPhone were new. Touchscreens, the mobile internet and music players all existed. Putting them in one thing that just worked was the invention. The parts of a colleague are on the table now. A voice that sounds like a person. A computer of its own. An inbox, a phone number and a calendar. A wallet, now that the card networks have started letting agents pay. A memory that outlasts every model. Nobody has put them in one body.

I think that body should look like a person, for the same reason Manpower worked. The org chart is the most widely deployed interface on Earth. Any company can take in a new person in a day, with an inbox, a calendar, a seat in the team chat and a manager. No company can take in a new category of software in a day. An agent with a name, a number and an inbox walks through the one door every company already has. Meta's Muse reached five million downloads in three weeks, faster than ChatGPT or Claude, and it lives inside WhatsApp, where people already talk. Instinct is the first product I have used that feels like someone working for me.

This has been tried badly. The last wave gave narrow bots human names and called them employees. A costume lasts a demo. A colleague lasts a year, because it has a computer of its own, a memory of everything it has done and a person it answers to.

Elon Musk's rule is that nobody switches for something twice as good. It has to be ten times better. Tools make a task faster, and that is the twice. The ten times is in what I call the delegation ratio, the hours of finished work you get back for each minute of your attention. You hand a tool a prompt and watch it work. You hand a colleague a job and check in on Friday.

That puts half the problem on the other side of the desk. The Enterprise is remembered for its captain as much as its crew. Every agent needs someone who briefs it, trusts it a little more each week and answers for it. Prompting is a skill few people have. Managing is one most working people already know, and it may be what finally opens AI to everyone else.

The hardest lesson I have learned building agents is that people cannot picture what something that can do anything will do for them. Nobody hires a person who can do anything. They hire an assistant, and a year later the office cannot run without them. So the general mind should arrive with a job title, and start where the work already runs through email and documents and every firm looks like the next.

There is an uncomfortable part. Since August, Europe's AI Act has required an AI that talks to people to tell them it is one. That is right. An agent can be shaped like a person and still be honest about what it is, and give a straight answer when asked. The harder question is work. Every new colleague changes the jobs around it, and this one never goes home. The honest answer I have is that every agent should report to a person, and that person should be the one whose job gets better.

In 1948 the product was a person who could walk into any office and start that morning. It is about to be again. The crew has been ready for a while. What is missing is the ship that carries it into the world, and it will look a lot like one of us.
