---
title: Superintelligence is a storage problem
date: 2026-10-05
summary: Which means separating the memory that lasts from the minds that come and go
plate: swell-cyan-3
---

In 1494 a Franciscan friar named Luca Pacioli published the method Venetian merchants had used for generations to keep their books. Double entry let a firm remember every coin that moved through it, better than any one of its partners could. Much of what a company is today grew out of that ledger. The firm became the thing that remembers.

The industry is betting that the next leap will come from a bigger model. I suspect that inside a company it will come from a better ledger. Every model is replaced within months, so what makes an agent brilliant at your company, who decides what, why the last deal fell through, where every file lives, has to sit in a record that outlasts every model it will ever use. Agents already pass the Turing test in writing and in voice. What they lack is what every new hire is given, a computer, an inbox, a calendar and years of memory.

The odd thing is that the right storage for this is the slowest in the cloud. For fifty years databases were built to answer in milliseconds, because a person was waiting. An agent waits seconds on a model at every step, so writing each step to object storage, which takes tens of milliseconds, adds about one percent. Speed stops mattering, and what is left is that nothing is lost and keeping it costs almost nothing. Neon put Postgres on object storage, turbopuffer did it for search, and workflow engines like Temporal write down every step so a crash can resume. Pull the thread through and you get agents whose minds are rented by the second and whose memory sits in a bucket that never fills up.

That split is more than engineering. It decides who holds the company's mind. When the memory lives inside a model vendor's product, the vendor keeps it and the company rents it back. When it lives in storage the company owns, the company keeps its mind and rents the thinking, the way it owns its books and hires its accountants. Most companies will make that choice without noticing, the day they switch on a memory feature.

It also changes what it means to work somewhere. The record keeps how you wrote, negotiated and decided, and when you leave, the agents keep doing your job the way you did it. Europe already lets you ask a company to erase your personal data. Nobody has written the rule for a company that has learned how you think. I expect employment contracts will soon say what a company may keep of you after you go, and I expect people will negotiate it the way they negotiate salary.

Run that for fifty years and a company becomes the judgment of everyone who ever worked there, still deciding after all of them are gone. Pacioli's ledger let merchants remember money. The record being written now will remember everything else, and the first company with no employees will be built from the memory of one that had them.
